# Evidence-based recipe principles for six audiences: gym-goers, post-workout recovery, bloating/IBS, iron-deficiency anaemia in women, pregnancy, and picky children (plus a bank of "mistake" hooks)

Research notes compiled 2026-11-13. Method note for the report writer: in this session the page fetcher was blocked for pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, ods.od.nih.gov, nhs.uk and monashfodmap.com, so the full texts were not opened. The figures below come from search-engine summaries of the primary pages (abstracts, guideline text and official pages), and the source URLs point at the primary page wherever one surfaced. Figures that rest only on secondary sites are flagged as such. Halal filter applied throughout: pork-based items in protein-quality tables are left out, and no alcohol-based ingredients are suggested.

Evidence-strength scale used: **Strong** (guideline or meta-analysis consensus, consistent RCTs) / **Moderate** (decent trials or lab data, some inconsistency or acute-only outcomes) / **Weak** (small, mixed, low-income-setting-only or mechanistic) / **Mostly hype** (popular claim contradicted or unsupported by the evidence).

## Q1. Gym-goers building muscle (high protein): how much, how often, when, and which halal sources

### Takeaway
Total daily protein of about 1.6 g/kg (ISSN range 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day) is the strongest lever. Spreading it as about 0.25-0.4 g/kg (roughly 20-40 g) per meal across 3-4+ meals is a reasonable, moderately supported refinement. The "30-minute anabolic window" is largely hype once total protein is matched. Eggs, dairy, chicken and beef are top-quality halal proteins. Lentils and chickpeas score lower and contain less leucine, so they need bigger portions or pairing.

### Cited Findings
- **ISSN Position Stand: Protein and Exercise (Jäger et al., JISSN 14:20, June 2017):** an overall daily intake of 1.4-2.0 g protein/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising individuals. Per serving: 0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein, or 20-40 g total. Acute doses should contain 700-3000 mg leucine and should ideally be spread evenly every 3-4 h across the day. Higher intakes (2.3-3.1 g/kg/day) may be needed to keep lean mass during calorie deficits in resistance-trained people. Pre-sleep casein (30-40 g) raises overnight muscle protein synthesis (MPS). — [DOAJ record, JISSN 2017](https://doaj.org/article/fcc34534a75d466aa37abda0042df7a7); [JISSN article, doi 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8); [NutraIngredients summary 2017](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2017/06/21/ISSN-publishes-position-stand-on-protein-and-exercise/)
- **Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med 52:376-384** (meta-analysis of 49 RCTs, n=1,863): protein supplementation added to resistance training increased 1RM strength by 2.49 kg and fat-free mass by 0.30 kg. Gains in fat-free mass plateaued at **1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03-2.20)**. The plateau applies to fat-free mass, not strength. Supplementation worked better in trained people and less well with increasing age. — [PMC full text (PMC5867436)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/display/publication763612)
- **Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, JISSN** ("How much protein can the body use in a single meal?"): target **0.4 g/kg/meal across a minimum of four meals** to reach at least 1.6 g/kg/day. Using the 2.2 g/kg/day upper figure gives a maximum of about 0.55 g/kg/meal. The paper notes digestion-rate differences: whey is absorbed at about 10 g/h, cooked egg protein at about 3 g/h. The authors say a single meal has a limited capacity to stimulate MPS (the "muscle-full" effect). — [PMC5828430](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430); [JISSN PDF](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1)
- **Caveat on the per-meal ceiling:** commentators (Henselmans) argue the four-meal minimum is less settled than the 0.4 g/kg figure suggests and that training may raise the per-meal ceiling. — [Menno Henselmans commentary](https://mennohenselmans.com/meal-frequency-science) (secondary, expert blog)
- **Protein timing / anabolic window (Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013 meta-analysis, JISSN):** the simple analysis showed a small hypertrophy effect from timed protein. In the full meta-regression controlling for covariates, there were **no significant differences** for strength or hypertrophy, and **total protein intake was the strongest predictor** of hypertrophy. The authors concluded there is no narrow "anabolic window of opportunity". — [PMC3879660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3879660)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (PeerJ):** pre- versus post-exercise protein had similar effects on muscular adaptations. — [PeerJ 2825](https://peerj.com/articles/2825)
- **ISSN nutrient-timing stand (Kerksick 2017):** high-quality protein eaten from immediately after to 2 h after exercise stimulates robust MPS increases. The size and timing of the pre-exercise meal affects how much the post-exercise feed matters. — [PMC5596471](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5596471/)
- **Protein quality (DIAAS) of halal sources.** Values vary between tables, so treat them as approximate. Beef about 111, chicken about 108, eggs about 113, whey protein isolate about 109 (all "high quality", ≥100). Chickpeas about 83 in one table and 67 in another. Red lentils about 50, yellow lentils about 73. Pea protein concentrate 82. Soy protein isolate about 90-91.5. — [ADPI Protein Quality Guide 2025](https://adpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/ADPI-Protein-Quality-Guide_2025.pdf); [secondary DIAAS table, Nutrition Advance](https://www.nutritionadvance.com/animal-protein-vs-plant-protein); [Food Label Maker DIAAS table](https://foodlabelmaker.com/blog/label-guide/pdcaas-protein-digestibility-diaas-of-common-foods/)
- **Leucine content:** whey is highest at about 13.6% leucine. Dairy is above 10%, other animal proteins about 8.5-9%, and most plant proteins about 6-8%. — [ADPI Protein Quality Guide 2025](https://adpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/ADPI-Protein-Quality-Guide_2025.pdf)
- **"Hidden" dairy protein:** plain non-fat Greek yogurt is about 10.2 g protein/100 g. Low-fat cottage cheese is about 11-12.4 g/100 g depending on the product (secondary sites citing USDA FoodData Central). — [Tastewise comparison](https://tastewise.io/blog/cottage-cheese-vs-greek-yogurt); [getfoodfacts, FDC 2705756](https://getfoodfacts.com/food/cheese-cottage-low-fat-2705756)
- **Regulatory note:** DIAAS is not yet adopted by regulators. PDCAAS is still the legally required method for protein claims in the US and Canada. — [Food Label Maker](https://foodlabelmaker.com/blog/label-guide/pdcaas-protein-digestibility-diaas-of-common-foods/)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Hit a meaningful protein dose per serving: about 25-40 g, or about 0.3-0.4 g/kg for the target eater.** Evidence: **Strong** for daily total (Morton 2018, ISSN 2017). **Moderate** for the per-meal figure (based on acute MPS work, Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018).
2. **Build around a high-DIAAS, leucine-rich halal anchor (eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, chicken, beef, fish).** For plant-based servings, use larger legume portions or pair legumes with dairy or eggs. Evidence: **Moderate-Strong** (DIAAS data are lab-based. Long-term outcomes with whole-food plant proteins at matched leucine are less studied).
3. **Frame the recipe as "one of your 3-4 protein meals today", not as a post-gym race.** Evidence: **Moderate** for distribution. Timing is **Mostly hype** as a strict "30-minute" rule.
4. **Optional evening dish: a dairy-based (casein-rich) snack such as cottage cheese or Greek yogurt.** Evidence: **Moderate** (acute overnight MPS. ISSN 2017 lists it, but it is a minor lever).

**Halal flags for this audience:** some flavoured yogurts and cottage cheeses contain gelatin, so the recipe should specify plain products or halal-certified ones. Whey or casein powders should be halal-certified, because some whey comes from cheese made with animal rennet. (This is a sourcing caution, not a cited fact.)

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "About 35 g protein per bowl, from eggs and Greek yogurt", "A high-protein lunch to help you reach your daily protein target", "Protein is one of the building blocks of muscle; pair it with training."
- Crosses the line: "Builds muscle fast", "anabolic meal", "burns fat while you sleep", "replaces your protein shake for gains", or any promised result in kg or a time frame.

### Gaps
- The exact wording and conditions of the EU/UK authorised claim for protein ("contributes to a growth in muscle mass / maintenance of muscle mass") were not retrieved in this session. Verify on the EU Health Claims Register before using it as on-pack-style wording.
- USDA FoodData Central was not reachable directly. Cottage cheese and Greek yogurt protein figures come from secondary sites citing USDA.
- Long-term RCTs comparing protein spread over 4+ meals with fewer larger meals at matched daily intake were not found. The evidence is mostly acute MPS.

## Q2. Post-workout recovery: carbohydrate + protein, glycogen, hydration; what is hype

### Takeaway
Protein (about 20-40 g) after training is sensible but not urgent. Adding carbohydrate does **not** raise muscle protein synthesis further when the protein dose is already adequate. Carbohydrate matters for **glycogen** when the next session is soon (under about 4 h apart) or for endurance or high-volume athletes. Rehydration of about 1.5 L of fluid per kg of body weight lost is a practical target from sports-nutrition bodies.

### Cited Findings
- **ISSN nutrient-timing stand (Kerksick et al., JISSN 2017):** for rapid glycogen restoration, it lists aggressive carbohydrate refeeding at **1.2 g/kg/h** (favouring high-GI sources), or carbohydrate at 0.8 g/kg/h plus protein at 0.2-0.4 g/kg/h. Carbohydrate alone or with protein during resistance exercise raises muscle glycogen and eases muscle damage. Post-exercise high-quality protein (immediately to 2 h after) stimulates MPS. — [PMC5596471](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5596471/)
- **Staples et al. 2011, Med Sci Sports Exerc** (n=9 men): 25 g whey vs 25 g whey + 50 g maltodextrin after resistance exercise. Carbohydrate raised glucose and insulin, but there was **no difference in MPS or muscle protein breakdown**. Insulin was "not additive or synergistic" when carbohydrate was eaten with a protein dose that already maximally stimulates MPS. — [PubMed 21131864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131864/)
- **Figueiredo & Cameron-Smith 2013 (JISSN review):** adding carbohydrate to 20-25 g of leucine-rich high-quality protein has no additive effect on MPS. **No chronic study** has compared protein + carbohydrate against protein alone for hypertrophy. — [JISSN PDF](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1550-2783-10-42)
- **Rehydration:** the German Nutrition Society (DGE) sports-nutrition working group recommends about **1.5 L of fluid per kg of weight lost** for quick and complete rehydration. — [DGE position, Ger J Sports Med 2020](https://www.germanjournalsportsmedicine.com/archive/archive-2020/issue-7-8-9/fluid-replacement-in-sports-position-of-the-working-group-sports-nutrition-of-the-german-nutrition-society-dge/)
- **ACSM 2007 fluid stand:** the goal is to prevent >2% body-weight loss from water deficit. Sweat rates vary, so people can estimate theirs by weighing before and after exercise. — [PubMed 17277604](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/)
- **2016 Joint Position (Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / Dietitians of Canada / ACSM; Thomas, Erdman & Burke):** nutrition goals should be periodised, and athletes should be referred to a dietitian for a personalised plan. — [Univ. of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/position-of-the-academy-of-nutrition-and-dietetics-dietitians-of-)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Combine about 20-40 g protein with a real carbohydrate portion (rice, potato, bread, fruit, dates)** for training days. The carbohydrate is there for glycogen and energy, not to "spike insulin for gains". Evidence: **Strong** for glycogen when sessions are close together or training is endurance or high-volume. **Moderate** that carbohydrate adds nothing to MPS (small acute studies).
2. **Include fluid and some salt (a salty, soupy or yogurt-based dish, or a drink alongside).** Evidence: **Moderate** (DGE 1.5 L/kg; ACSM advises individual sweat-rate checks).
3. **Present "eat a balanced meal within a couple of hours" rather than "30 minutes or you lose gains".** Evidence: **Mostly hype** for the strict window (Schoenfeld 2013; Q1).

**What is hype:** the strict 30-minute window; "insulin spike" sugar added to protein shakes for muscle growth; recovery "superfoods" presented as reducing soreness. The last one was not researched here, so make no claims about it.

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "A post-training plate with 30 g protein and slow-cooked rice to refuel", "Good for training days", "Carbs refill the energy your muscles use."
- Crosses the line: "Repairs muscle damage", "reduces inflammation", "speeds recovery by X%", "prevents soreness".

### Gaps
- The 2016 ACSM/AND/DC figures (carbohydrate 1.0-1.2 g/kg/h for 4 h; fluid 125-150% of losses) could not be verified from the retrieved text. The DGE 1.5 L/kg figure is the verified substitute.
- The exact protein:carbohydrate "3:1 or 4:1" ratio guidance was not confirmed in retrieved text. Avoid quoting a ratio.
- No sodium target for post-exercise rehydration was verified. The ACSM 1996 0.5-0.7 g/L figure is superseded.

## Q3. Bloating and IBS (gut health): low-FODMAP, NICE/ACG guidance, fibre type, garlic oil, legumes, fermented foods

### Takeaway
The strongest food-level moves are: **soluble fibre (oats, psyllium/ispaghula) instead of insoluble bran**; **garlic- or onion-infused oil instead of the bulbs** (fructans are water-soluble, not oil-soluble); and **small portions of canned, rinsed and drained lentils or chickpeas**. The low-FODMAP diet itself is a dietitian-led elimination and reintroduction process with low-quality evidence, not something one recipe "does". Fermented foods have weak evidence and do not reduce bloating scores in the best meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **NICE CG61 (IBS in adults; dietary recommendations from 2008, updated 2015; NICE has decided not to update the clinical-management section):** review fibre intake and adjust it, usually reducing it, while monitoring symptoms. **Discourage insoluble fibre (e.g. bran).** If more fibre is needed, use **soluble fibre such as ispaghula powder or foods high in soluble fibre (e.g. oats)**. It may help to limit high-fibre foods such as wholemeal or high-fibre flour and bread, bran cereals and whole grains such as brown rice. — [NICE CG61 Recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg61/chapter/Recommendations)
- **ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of IBS (Lacy et al., 2021):** suggests **soluble, not insoluble, fibre** for global IBS symptoms. The grading table lists it as a strong recommendation on moderate-quality evidence. Soluble sources: psyllium, oat bran, barley, beans. Insoluble: wheat bran, whole grains, some vegetables. Bran did not appear to help. **Low-FODMAP:** a limited trial is recommended to improve global symptoms, a **conditional recommendation on low-quality (some summaries say very low) evidence**, ideally with a trained GI dietitian. — [ACG magazine summary PDF](https://webfiles.gi.org/docs/publications/ibs_guideline_acg_magazine.pdf); [NursingCenter guideline summary, Oct 2021](https://nursingcenter.com/clinical-resources/guideline-summaries/irritable-bowel-syndrome-acg-clinical-guideline-on); [Guideline Central](https://guidelinecentral.com/guideline/309975)
- **Monash University on onion and garlic:** fructans dissolve in water. Putting a whole onion or garlic clove into a soup or stock and removing it before eating **does not work**, because fructans have already leached into the liquid. In an oil-based dish the fructans do not leach into the oil. Monash "can't recommend the use of cooking to lower FODMAP levels" because more research is needed. — [Monash FODMAP blog: Cooking with onion and garlic, myths and facts](https://monashfodmap.com/blog/cooking-with-onion-and-garlic-myths-and/)
- **Garlic-infused oil:** fructans are water-soluble only, so Monash says garlic-infused oil should be well tolerated by most people on a low-FODMAP diet (suggested serving about 1 tbsp). Products with **visible garlic pieces are not low-FODMAP**. Infused oil stays low-FODMAP only if no water-based ingredients (stock, tomatoes, water) go into the infusion step. — [FODMAP Everyday: garlic](https://www.fodmapeveryday.com/ingredients/garlic); [Healthy Food: garlic and low FODMAP](https://www.healthyfood.com/advice/garlic-and-a-low-fodmap-diet/); [FODMAP Everyday reply quoted in Monash-related search](https://www.fodmapeveryday.com/get-your-garlic-fix/)
- **Canned legumes:** rinsing and draining canned lentils lowers FODMAP content because the water-soluble GOS leaches into the can liquid. The low-FODMAP serving for canned lentils is **¼ cup (46 g)**. Monash cut it from ½ cup to ¼ cup in **2024**. Canned chickpeas: ¼ cup (42 g), rinsed and drained. These are secondary sites quoting Monash, so check the current Monash app. — [A Little Bit Yummy: canned foods and FODMAPs](https://alittlebityummy.com/blog/canned-foods-fodmaps-whats-the-story/); [FODMAP Everyday: roasted chickpeas](https://www.fodmapeveryday.com/recipes/roasted-chickpeas/)
- **Fermented foods (Ding et al., Frontiers in Nutrition, Jan 2025; meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, n=1,264):** symptom relief RR 1.22 (95% CI 1.04-1.42) across 12 RCTs. Only the **fermented-milk** subgroup was significant. There was **no significant improvement in abdominal pain or bloating scores**. The authors say results "should be interpreted with caution". — [Frontiers in Nutrition 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1494118/full)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Flavour with garlic- or onion-infused oil (strained, no pieces), or with the green parts of spring onion or chives, instead of whole garlic or onion.** Evidence: **Strong** that the oil is low-FODMAP (Monash lab analysis). The benefit for any individual depends on whether fructans are their trigger.
2. **Use soluble-fibre bases (oats, or a psyllium/ispaghula-thickened bake) and avoid adding wheat bran.** Evidence: **Strong** (NICE CG61; ACG 2021 strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).
3. **If using legumes, use canned, rinsed and drained lentils or chickpeas in a measured small portion (about ¼ cup per serve).** Evidence: **Moderate** (Monash lab testing via secondary reporting; serving sizes change).
4. **Avoid the "fish it out" trick in soups and stews.** Water-based dishes carry the fructans. Evidence: **Strong** (Monash).

**Do not lean on:** "gut-healing" fermented foods for bloating. The evidence is **Weak** (modest global benefit for fermented milk only; no effect on bloating).

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "Made with garlic-infused oil instead of garlic cloves, a swap used on the low-FODMAP diet", "Lower-FODMAP version: canned lentils, rinsed, ¼ cup per serve", "If you have IBS, the low-FODMAP diet works best with a dietitian."
- Crosses the line: "Cures bloating", "heals your gut", "IBS-safe", "fixes IBS", "anti-bloat recipe", "Monash-certified" (unless it is certified).

### Gaps
- NICE CG61 points on fresh fruit (about 3 portions/day), linseed and fluid/caffeine advice were not confirmed in retrieved text (the NICE page could be summarised but not fetched).
- No British Dietetic Association statement on fermented foods was retrieved.
- No direct raw-versus-cooked fructan comparison for onion or garlic was found. Monash says cooking is not a recommended way to lower FODMAPs.
- No source verified a psyllium gram dose. The safety rules for home-made garlic oil (botulism risk with garlic in oil stored at room temperature) were not retrieved. Treat "make fresh, refrigerate, use quickly" as a gap needing a food-safety source before giving storage advice.

## Q4. Iron-deficiency anaemia in women: haem vs non-haem, vitamin C, tea/coffee/calcium, cast iron, soaking/sprouting

### Takeaway
The best-supported recipe moves are: include **haem iron (red meat, poultry, fish)**, or pair **non-haem iron (lentils, chickpeas, beans, fortified grains) with vitamin C-rich foods**; and serve **tea or coffee away from the meal** (by about 1 h). Calcium's single-meal effect is real but has little long-term effect on iron status. Cast iron is weak evidence. Spinach is a poor iron source despite its reputation. Food cannot be presented as treating diagnosed anaemia.

### Cited Findings
- **Scale of the problem (WHO Global Health Observatory):** **30.7% of women aged 15-49** had anaemia in 2023, and **35.5% of pregnant women**. Prevalence rose from 27.6% (2012). Iron deficiency is the leading cause. — [WHO GHO: anaemia in women and children](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/themes/topics/anaemia_in_women_and_children); [WHO news, May 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-05-2023-who-calls-for-accelerated-action-to-reduce-anaemia)
- **NIH ODS iron fact sheet:** haem iron from meat is more bioavailable than non-haem iron from plants, and meat, poultry and seafood **also increase non-haem iron absorption**. Calcium might reduce the bioavailability of both non-haem and haem iron, so take calcium and iron supplements at different times. Polyphenols inhibit absorption, and **spinach has low iron bioavailability because of inhibitors such as polyphenols**. RDA in pregnancy is **27 mg/day**. — [ODS Iron, Health Professional](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/); [ODS Iron, Consumer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/iron-Consumer/)
- **Coffee and tea (Morck et al. 1983, Am J Clin Nutr):** a cup of coffee reduced iron absorption from a hamburger meal by **39%**, compared with **64% for tea**. **No decrease** occurred when coffee was drunk **1 h before** the meal. — [PubMed 6402915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6402915/)
- **Tea effect size varies with strength and brew:** secondary sources report 26-99% inhibition depending on brew. The effect comes from polyphenols/tannins, not caffeine. A 1-hour gap attenuates the effect (2017 trial cited secondarily). — [The Conversation](https://Theconversation.com/what-to-drink-with-dinner-to-get-the-most-iron-from-your-food-and-what-to-avoid-156579); [The Gut Health Doctor](https://theguthealthdoctor.com/tea-iron-absorption) (secondary)
- **Vitamin C (EFSA 2009, EFSA Journal 7(9):1226):** EFSA established a cause-and-effect relationship between dietary vitamin C and **increased non-haem iron absorption**. It is on the EU authorised list as "**Vitamin C increases iron absorption**". The food must be at least a "source of" vitamin C to bear the claim. — [EFSA Journal 1226](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/1226)
- **Vitamin C caveat (Cook & Reddy 2001, Am J Clin Nutr):** over a complete diet (several meals across 5-day periods), absorption did **not** differ significantly across wide vitamin C intakes. The enhancing effect is **far less pronounced than in single-meal studies**, which may explain why prolonged vitamin C supplementation often does not change iron status. — [PubMed 11124756](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11124756/)
- **Calcium (Hallberg et al. 1991):** in single meals, non-haem iron absorption fell by **50-60% at 300-600 mg calcium**, with no further inhibition above about 300 mg. **Multiple-meal and long-term studies show much smaller effects**: calcium supplementation had no effect on iron status in adolescent girls, adult men and women, or lactating women. A review notes a possible small effect in pregnancy if iron supplements are not taken. — [Hallberg 1991 (AGRIS record)](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de38d263b8185d9ca7afd3); [Nutrition Research Reviews 2000 review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0954422400000706); [PubMed 19087437: The effect of calcium on iron absorption](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19087437/)
- **Cast iron / iron cookware:** Alves et al. 2019 (PLoS ONE, systematic review of 11 studies, low- and middle-income countries): significant haemoglobin or iron-index increases in **4 of 8 pot studies** and 1 of 3 ingot studies. A possible benefit with consistent use, mainly in children. Sharma et al. 2021: 4 of 13 studies showed significant haemoglobin gains. Geerligs 2003: limited evidence. A 2023 clinical commentary concludes evidence is **insufficient to use iron cookware to treat iron-deficiency anaemia**. — [Alves 2019, PMC6719866](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6719866/); [Sharma 2021, PMC8266402](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8266402/); [Geerligs 2003, PubMed 12859709](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12859709/); [Clinical Correlations 2023](https://www.clinicalcorrelations.org/2023/08/31/should-we-be-prescribing-cookware-a-potential-intervention-for-iron-deficiency-anemia/)
- **Soaking and sprouting legumes:** in mungbean, chickpea and cowpea, phytate fell **5.3-16.1% with soaking** and **25.7-46.4% with germination**. Predicted iron availability (phytate:iron ratio) improved with longer germination. **Soaking alone can leach iron into the soaking water**, so soaking combined with fermentation or germination is needed to improve iron and zinc bioavailability. A household-processing review (Haileslassie et al., IJFST) found significant but variable reductions and no consensus on optimal conditions. — [Haileslassie et al., IJFST](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/ijfs.13166); [J Food Sci Technol (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13197-011-0582-y) (abstract-level summaries; exact figures need checking against the full papers)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Pair a non-haem iron base (lentils, chickpeas, beans, fortified grains) with a vitamin C food in the same dish** (lemon juice, tomatoes, peppers, parsley, citrus). Evidence: **Strong** for single-meal absorption (EFSA-authorised wording). **Moderate** for effect on iron status over time (Cook & Reddy 2001).
2. **Add some haem iron when the recipe allows (beef, lamb, chicken thigh, sardines, tuna).** This adds well-absorbed iron and boosts absorption of plant iron in the same meal. Evidence: **Strong** (ODS).
3. **Serve tea or coffee about an hour away from the meal, not with it.** Evidence: **Strong** for single-meal absorption (coffee -39%, tea -64%, Morck 1983). Long-term status effect is less certain.
4. **Optional: sprouted lentils or chickpeas, or soaking plus sprouting.** Evidence: **Moderate** for phytate reduction. **Weak** for human iron status.

**Do not lean on:** "cook in cast iron to fix anaemia" (**Weak**, mainly low-income settings with mixed results); "never put cheese or yogurt near iron" (single-meal effect real but long-term effect small: **Weak** as a practical rule); "spinach is the iron king" (**Mostly hype**; low bioavailability per ODS).

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "Lentils plus lemon: vitamin C increases iron absorption", "An iron-rich dinner idea", "Have your tea an hour after, not with the meal", "If you think you're low in iron, get a blood test; food helps, but diagnosed anaemia needs your doctor."
- Crosses the line: "Cures anaemia", "raises your haemoglobin", "replaces iron tablets", "fixes fatigue", "anaemia-fighting recipe".

### Gaps
- The ODS page could not be opened, so the **18 mg/day RDA for women 19-50**, the 1.8× multiplier for vegetarians, the per-food iron table and the haem/non-haem absorption percentages (often quoted as about 15-35% vs 2-20%) were **not verified** in this session.
- The original trial for "100 mg vitamin C quadruples absorption" was not retrieved (secondary only). Avoid quoting a multiplier.
- The Hurrell 1999 polyphenol-beverage study figures were not retrieved directly.
- WHO's current anaemia fact sheet text (food-based recommendations) was not retrieved, only GHO prevalence data.

## Q5. Pregnancy: foods to favour, foods to avoid, food safety, gestational diabetes basics

### Takeaway
In pregnancy, **food safety rules matter more than "superfoods"**. Avoid liver and pâté (vitamin A), unpasteurised and mould-ripened or blue soft cheese unless cooked until steaming hot, raw or partly cooked eggs except British Lion or Laid in Britain eggs in the UK, shark, swordfish and marlin, and keep caffeine under 200 mg/day. Positive nutrients a recipe can feature: **choline (eggs), iodine (dairy, fish), DHA from low-mercury oily fish (max 2 portions/week in UK guidance), iron and folate-rich foods**. Food alone is not enough for folate: a **400 mcg folic acid supplement** is advised until 12 weeks. For gestational diabetes, the advice is starchy carbohydrate at each meal in **controlled portions, choosing higher-fibre, low-GI options**.

### Cited Findings
- **Folic acid (NHS):** 400 microgram tablet daily, starting before pregnancy and continuing until 12 weeks. It is difficult to get enough folate from food alone. — [NHS: vitamins, supplements and nutrition in pregnancy](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/vitamins-supplements-and-nutrition/)
- **Vitamin D (NHS):** 10 micrograms/day supplement. More than 100 micrograms/day can be harmful. — [NHS: vitamins and supplements](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/vitamins-supplements-and-nutrition/)
- **Liver and vitamin A (NHS):** avoid liver and liver products such as pâté. Avoid cod liver oil and any supplement containing vitamin A (retinol), because too much vitamin A could harm the baby. — [NHS: foods to avoid in pregnancy](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/)
- **Fish (NHS):** no more than 2 portions of oily fish a week. Avoid shark, swordfish and marlin. — [NHS: have a healthy diet in pregnancy](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/have-a-healthy-diet/)
- **Caffeine (NHS):** under 200 mg/day, roughly 2 mugs of instant coffee or 1 mug of filter coffee. — [NHS Lothian leaflet, 2023](https://policyonline.nhslothian.scot/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Pregnancy_and_nutrition.pdf)
- **Cheese (NHS / NI Direct):** soft or blue cheese, pasteurised or unpasteurised, is OK **only when cooked until steaming hot**. Avoid mould-ripened cheeses (Brie, Camembert, mould-ripened goat's cheese) and blue-veined cheeses uncooked, because of listeria. **Pasteurised soft cheeses are fine:** cottage cheese, mozzarella, feta, cream cheese, paneer, ricotta, halloumi. — [NHS foods to avoid](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/); [NI Direct: foods to avoid in pregnancy](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/foods-avoid-pregnancy)
- **Eggs (UK):** raw or lightly cooked hen eggs are OK only if **British Lion** or produced under the **Laid in Britain** scheme. Other hen eggs should be well cooked. Avoid raw or partly cooked duck, goose or quail eggs. — [NI Direct](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/foods-avoid-pregnancy); [NHS foods to avoid](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/)
- **Listeria:** listeriosis can cause miscarriage or stillbirth, or make a newborn seriously ill. Avoid ready-to-eat cold-smoked or cured fish and cold cured ready-to-eat meats. — [NI Direct](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/foods-avoid-pregnancy)
- **Choline (NIH ODS):** **450 mg/day** in pregnancy (ages 19-50). Most prenatal vitamins contain little or no choline. — [NIH ODS: Dietary supplements and life stages, pregnancy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy/); [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/medical-services/birthplace/pregnancy-newborn-health/prenatal-education/your-pregnancy/nutrition-during-pregnancy)
- **Iodine:** **220 mcg/day** in pregnancy. Some prenatal vitamins contain none. — [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/medical-services/birthplace/pregnancy-education/prenatal-education/your-pregnancy/nutrition-during-pregnancy); [Babylist](https://babylist.com/hello-baby/pregnancy-nutrients) (secondary)
- **Fish (US guidance):** **8-12 oz/week of lower-mercury fish**. Avoid shark and swordfish. Some sources cap albacore tuna at 6 oz/week. — [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/medical-services/birthplace/pregnancy-newborn-health/prenatal-education/your-pregnancy/nutrition-during-pregnancy); [Lamaze](https://lamaze.org/Giving-Birth-with-Confidence/nutrition-during-pregnancy)
- **Iron in pregnancy:** RDA 27 mg/day (NIH ODS). 35.5% of pregnant women worldwide are anaemic (WHO 2023). — [ODS Iron Consumer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/iron-Consumer/); [WHO GHO](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/themes/topics/anaemia_in_women_and_children)
- **Gestational diabetes (NHS trust dietetic leaflets, 2024-2025):** three meals a day, each with a starchy carbohydrate, choosing higher-fibre, lower-GI versions (wholegrains, pulses, porridge oats rather than instant, basmati or brown rice, new or sweet potatoes). Avoid added sugars. Some trusts limit snacks to ≤10 g carbohydrate. **Low-GI foods still raise blood glucose, so portions matter** (e.g. 1 fist-sized potato, 1 handful of uncooked rice or pasta). — [Sandwell & West Birmingham NHS, 2024](https://www.swbh.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Dietary-advice-for-the-management-ML6731.pdf); [University Hospitals Sussex, 2024](https://www.uhsussex.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Healthy-eating-with-gestational-diabetes4-1.pdf); [University Hospitals Leicester, 2025](https://www.uhleicester.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/808_082023-Food-choices-when-you-have-diabetes-during-pregnancy.pdf); [Tommy's: GI and gestational diabetes](https://www.tommys.org/pregnancy-information/complications/gestational-diabetes/treatment-glycaemic-index)
- **ACOG 2026 resource** mentions newer guidance on high-dose omega-3 supplementation to reduce preterm-birth risk but gives no figure in the retrieved text. — [CRN-hosted ACOG 2026 prenatal resource](https://crnusa.org/sites/default/files/pdfs/Prenatal-resource-ACOG-2026-(FINAL).pdf)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Food safety first:** fully cooked eggs (or UK British Lion eggs if runny), fully cooked meat, cheese either pasteurised-and-safe (feta, halloumi, paneer, mozzarella, cottage cheese) or cooked until steaming hot, no liver or pâté, no cold-smoked fish. Evidence: **Strong** (NHS guideline).
2. **Feature eggs (choline), dairy or fish (iodine) and low-mercury oily fish such as salmon or sardines (DHA, ≤2 oily-fish portions/week in UK).** Evidence: **Strong** for intake targets as guideline values. **Moderate** for outcome claims, so make none.
3. **Build an iron-plus-vitamin C plate** (see Q4), and folate-rich foods (pulses, leafy greens). Never imply food replaces the folic acid supplement. Evidence: **Strong** (NHS says food alone is not enough).
4. **For a gestational-diabetes-friendly version:** a measured portion of low-GI starchy carbohydrate (basmati or brown rice, oats, pulses) with protein and vegetables, and no added sugar. Evidence: **Moderate-Strong** (NHS trust dietetic consensus). Portions vary by trust and individual.

**Halal note:** liver (a common halal iron food) must be flagged as **avoid in pregnancy**. This is a strong "mistake" hook.

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "Pregnancy-friendly: follows NHS food-safety advice (cheese cooked till steaming, eggs fully set)", "Eggs are a source of choline", "Talk to your midwife about supplements; folic acid is recommended until 12 weeks."
- Crosses the line: "Prevents birth defects", "boosts baby's brain/IQ", "prevents gestational diabetes", "safe for gestational diabetes" (blood-glucose responses are individual), "pregnancy superfood".

### Gaps
- The full NHS "foods to avoid" page could not be opened. Herbal tea guidance and current wording on tuna limits were not verified.
- No ACOG document was retrieved directly. The 8-12 oz fish figure is from UCLA Health citing US guidance. DHA mg targets disagree between secondary sources (200-300 vs 300-400 mg/day), so do not quote a number.
- WHO antenatal care recommendations (2016) on daily iron and folic acid were not retrieved.
- UK-specific British Lion egg advice does not apply to eggs in other countries (e.g. Middle East or Türkiye audiences). Wording for non-UK audiences should default to "fully cooked eggs".

## Q6. Busy mothers feeding picky children: repeated exposure, involving kids, hidden vegetables, sugar limits

### Takeaway
**Repeated, visible exposure** is the best-supported way to raise children's liking of vegetables. Acceptance often needs about 8-15 tastes, but parents typically give up after 3-5. **Hidden vegetables raise intake** (Spill 2011: +50-73% vegetable intake, about 12% fewer calories) but are best used **alongside** visible exposure, not instead of it. **Involving children in cooking** increased vegetable intake in a controlled study. Sugar: AHA says under 25 g/day added sugar for ages 2-18 and none under age 2. WHO says free sugars under 10% of energy (strong) and ideally under 5% (conditional).

### Cited Findings
- **Repeated exposure, number of tastes:** reviews cite **up to 10-15 taste exposures** (Carruth 2004 cited for 10-15). Other work finds 8-10. An infant study found that by the **8th offering**, intake of a previously rejected vegetable matched a preferred one. Parents typically offer a food only **3-5 times** before giving up. A 2021 study (159 children aged 3-6) found all exposure frequencies tested were effective. — [City, University of London open-access review (Appetite 2022)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29925/1/1-s2.0-S0195666322005384-main.pdf); [Alimentarium: Persistence pays](https://www.alimentarium.org/en/magazine/science/persistence-pays); [White Rose eprint (J Acad Nutr Diet)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/118792/1/1-s2.0-S2212267213012549-main.pdf) (search summaries did not attribute each figure to an exact paper. See Gaps.)
- **Familiarity:** a 2007 review concluded that familiarity is perhaps the most important determinant of a child's liking for a food. — [PubMed 17635306: The importance of exposure for healthy eating in childhood](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17635306/)
- **Systematic review, infants and toddlers ≤3 y (Barends et al. 2019):** repeated exposure increased acceptance of the target vegetable. **Exposure to variety** was particularly effective for accepting a *new* vegetable. **Visual exposure alone** increased acceptance. — [White Rose eprint 143246](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/143246/)
- **Hidden vegetables (Spill et al. 2011, Am J Clin Nutr; crossover, 40 children aged 3-5):** pureed vegetables added to entrées (tripled and quadrupled vegetable content) **raised vegetable intake by 52 g (50%) and 73 g (73%)** and **lowered daily energy intake by 142 kcal (12%)**. Liking ratings were similar across versions. Lead author: hidden vegetables should be **used with other strategies** such as vegetables as snacks and sides. — [PMC3155937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155937); [ScienceDaily 2011](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/07/110725123557.htm)
- **Labelled vs unlabelled hidden-veg foods (2012 pilot):** children's liking did not differ when told the bread or cake contained zucchini or broccoli. — [ScienceDaily 2012](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/03/120301180910.htm)
- **Involving children in cooking (van der Horst, Ferrage & Rytz 2014, Appetite 79:18-24; children aged 6-10):** children who helped a parent cook lunch ate **more vegetables (salad +76.1%)** and more of the meal overall than children who did not help. The authors caution about portion sizes. **Funded and run by the Nestlé Research Centre.** — [Nestlé Australia media summary](https://www.nestle.com.au/en/media/children-who-cook-eat-more-greens); [New Food Magazine](https://newfoodmagazine.com/?p=13744)
- **Cooking programmes (2024 systematic review of RCTs):** the most frequent positive outcomes were cooking-skill knowledge, self-efficacy and child cooking involvement. Dietary-intake effects were less consistent. — [BFH arbor PDF (Discover Social Science and Health 2023/24)](https://arbor.bfh.ch/19425/1/s44155-023-00042-4.pdf)
- **AHA (Vos et al. 2016 scientific statement):** children aged 2-18 should have **<25 g (about 6 tsp) added sugar/day** and **≤8 oz sugar-sweetened beverages per week**. **Under 2s: avoid added sugars.** — [The Hospitalist summary](https://community.the-hospitalist.org/content/aha-limit-childrens-added-sugar-intake-25-g/day); [Safety+Health summary](https://www.safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/14621-heart-association-releases-guidelines-on-kids-and-added-sugars)
- **WHO Guideline on sugars (2015):** free sugars **<10% of energy (strong recommendation)** and **<5% (conditional)**. "Free sugars" include honey, syrups and fruit juices. — [WHO news release, March 2015](https://who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children)

### Inferences
**Principles a single recipe can honestly apply:**
1. **Keep at least one visible, recognisable vegetable on the plate even if others are blended in ("show one, hide one").** Evidence: **Strong** for repeated exposure. **Moderate** that hiding raises intake (single lab crossover, short-term).
2. **Design one kid task into the recipe (washing, tearing, sprinkling, assembling).** Evidence: **Moderate** (one controlled study, industry-funded; supporting review).
3. **Blend vegetables into sauces, meatballs or batters to raise vegetable volume and lower calorie density.** Evidence: **Moderate** (Spill 2011).
4. **Sweeten lightly or with fruit; keep added sugar per portion well under the 25 g/day budget, and use no honey or added sugar for under-2s.** Evidence: **Strong** (WHO <10%). The AHA figure is guideline consensus. Note that honey and fruit juice count as free sugars under WHO.

**Safe wording vs crossing the line:**
- Safe: "Three vegetables blended into the sauce, plus one on top so they get to see it", "It can take 8-15 tries before a child accepts a new vegetable, so keep offering", "Let them sprinkle the cheese; kids who help cook often eat more veg."
- Crosses the line: "Cures picky eating", "your kids will love vegetables", "guaranteed clean plates", "boosts immunity or brain development", "sugar-free" (if it contains honey, dates or juice; WHO counts these as free sugars).

### Gaps
- The search summaries did not attach each repeated-exposure statistic to a specific paper, so the 8-15 range is a defensible summary, not one study's result. Caton et al. 2013 ("Repetition counts") was identified but not retrieved.
- No AAP picky-eating guidance was retrieved. No long-term evidence was found on whether relying on hidden vegetables delays acceptance of visible vegetables. This is commonly asserted, but the only direct data found was a small 2012 pilot.
- The UK SACN/NHS free-sugar limits for children by age (e.g. max 19 g/day at 4-6 y, 24 g at 7-10 y) were not retrieved in this session.

## Q7. "Mistake" hook bank (counterintuitive food-science facts for recipe hooks), ranked by evidence, with safe phrasing

### Takeaway
The safest and strongest hooks are **tea with your lentils**, **squeezing lemon on legumes**, **fishing the garlic out of soup doesn't work**, **garlic in oil, not in water**, **liver in pregnancy**, **giving up on a vegetable after 3 tries**, **bran for bloating** and **the 30-minute protein panic**. Several popular "mistakes" (cast iron, cheese blocking iron, fermented foods for bloating) are overstated and should be avoided or softened.

### Cited Findings
(Each item gives the hook, the fact behind it and the evidence rating. Sources are the same as in Q1-Q6.)
- **"Drinking tea with your iron-rich lentils."** Tea cut iron absorption from a meal by 64% and coffee by 39%. Coffee 1 h before the meal had no effect. **Strong** (single-meal). — [Morck 1983, PubMed 6402915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6402915/)
- **"Skipping the lemon."** Vitamin C increases non-haem iron absorption (EFSA-authorised). **Strong**. The long-term effect on status is smaller, so do not promise a multiplier. — [EFSA 1226](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/1226); [Cook & Reddy 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11124756/)
- **"Thinking spinach is your iron source."** Spinach iron has low bioavailability because of polyphenols. **Moderate-Strong**. — [ODS Iron HP](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/)
- **"Taking the garlic out of the soup doesn't save your gut."** Fructans leach into water. **Strong** (Monash). — [Monash blog](https://monashfodmap.com/blog/cooking-with-onion-and-garlic-myths-and/)
- **"Garlic flavour without the garlic."** Fructans are not fat-soluble, so strained garlic-infused oil is low-FODMAP. **Strong**. — [FODMAP Everyday: garlic](https://www.fodmapeveryday.com/ingredients/garlic)
- **"Not rinsing canned chickpeas or lentils."** GOS leach into the canning liquid. Rinsing and a ¼-cup serve keep them low-FODMAP. **Moderate**. — [A Little Bit Yummy](https://alittlebityummy.com/blog/canned-foods-fodmaps-whats-the-story/)
- **"Adding bran when you're bloated."** NICE discourages insoluble fibre (bran) in IBS. Oats and psyllium are the soluble alternatives. **Strong**. — [NICE CG61](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg61/chapter/Recommendations); [ACG 2021](https://webfiles.gi.org/docs/publications/ibs_guideline_acg_magazine.pdf)
- **"Eating all your protein at dinner."** About 0.4 g/kg across ≥4 meals is suggested. **Moderate**. — [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430)
- **"Panicking about the 30-minute window."** Once total protein is controlled, timing effects vanish. Pre- and post-workout protein work similarly. The real "mistake" is low daily protein. **Moderate-Strong**. — [Schoenfeld 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3879660); [PeerJ 2017](https://peerj.com/articles/2825)
- **"Adding sugar to your shake to 'spike insulin' for muscle."** With 25 g whey, adding 50 g carbohydrate did not raise MPS. **Moderate** (small acute study). Carbohydrate still matters for glycogen. — [Staples 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131864/)
- **"Ignoring cottage cheese and Greek yogurt as protein."** About 10-12 g protein/100 g. **Moderate** (secondary USDA-citing data; check labels for halal gelatin). — [Tastewise](https://tastewise.io/blog/cottage-cheese-vs-greek-yogurt)
- **"Thinking lentils equal steak for protein quality."** Lentil DIAAS is about 50-73 vs about 108-113 for chicken, beef and eggs, and lentils have lower leucine. **Moderate** (values vary by table). — [ADPI 2025](https://adpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/ADPI-Protein-Quality-Guide_2025.pdf)
- **"Only soaking your beans."** Soaking removes about 5-16% of phytate (and can leach iron). Sprouting removes about 26-46%. **Moderate** for phytate, **Weak** for iron status. — [Springer J Food Sci Technol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13197-011-0582-y); [IJFST review](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/ijfs.13166)
- **"Eating liver for iron while pregnant."** The NHS says avoid liver and pâté in pregnancy (vitamin A). **Strong**. — [NHS foods to avoid](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/)
- **"Saying no to all soft cheese in pregnancy."** Pasteurised feta, halloumi, paneer, mozzarella, ricotta and cottage cheese are fine. Brie, Camembert and blue cheese are fine only cooked until steaming hot. **Strong**. — [NI Direct](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/foods-avoid-pregnancy)
- **"Giving up after 3 tries."** Acceptance often takes about 8-15 exposures, but parents usually stop at 3-5. **Strong** (direction). The number varies. — [City Univ. open-access review](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29925/1/1-s2.0-S0195666322005384-main.pdf)
- **"Only hiding the vegetables."** Hiding raised intake by 50-73%, but researchers advise pairing it with visible vegetables. **Moderate**. — [Spill 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155937)
- **"Keeping kids out of the kitchen."** Kids who cooked ate 76% more salad. **Moderate** (one Nestlé-run study). — [Nestlé summary of van der Horst 2014](https://www.nestle.com.au/en/media/children-who-cook-eat-more-greens)

### Inferences
**Hooks to avoid or soften (overstated):**
- "Cook in cast iron to fix your iron." **Weak** (mixed results, low-income settings, not a treatment). — [Clinical Correlations 2023](https://www.clinicalcorrelations.org/2023/08/31/should-we-be-prescribing-cookware-a-potential-intervention-for-iron-deficiency-anemia/)
- "Dairy ruins your iron meal." The single-meal effect is real (50-60% at 300-600 mg Ca), but there is little long-term effect on status. Use only as "if your iron is low, consider having the big glass of milk at a different meal", not as a "mistake". — [Nutr Res Rev 2000](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0954422400000706)
- "Fermented foods fix bloating." **Weak**, with no effect on bloating scores in the 2025 meta-analysis. — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1494118/full)
- "Cooking the onion makes it IBS-safe." Not supported (Monash). — [Monash blog](https://monashfodmap.com/blog/cooking-with-onion-and-garlic-myths-and/)

**General wording rule (for all audiences):** describe **what is in the food and what it does in the meal** (e.g. "vitamin C increases iron absorption", "30 g protein per serving", "made with garlic-infused oil", "follows NHS pregnancy food-safety advice"). Never state that a recipe **prevents, treats or cures** a condition (anaemia, IBS, gestational diabetes, picky eating) or promises a body outcome (muscle gain in kg, recovery speed, baby's IQ). Add a "speak to your doctor, dietitian or midwife" line for anaemia, IBS and pregnancy content.

### Gaps
- The exact EU/UK regulatory text banning disease prevention/treatment/cure claims for foods (Regulation (EU) 1169/2011 art. 7; Regulation 1924/2006) was not retrieved in this session. The wording rule above follows the brief's constraint and standard practice, not a quoted regulation.
- Health-claim rules in the creator's other likely markets (e.g. Türkiye, Gulf states) were not researched.
- The fetch-blocked primary pages (PMC, NIH ODS, NHS, Monash) should be spot-checked by a human for exact numbers before publishing statistics on screen. They can be opened in this environment only once those hosts are allowed in the session's network settings.
